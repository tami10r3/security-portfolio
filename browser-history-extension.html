Browser Extension CF-03

History Digest

A Manifest V3 Chrome extension that turns your browsing history into an AI-generated activity summary — built for logbook-style reporting, with a Q&A mode and PDF export.

Overview

History Digest reads your Chrome browsing history over a selected window — 24 hours, 3 days, 7 days, or 30 days — and sends a summarized version of it to Claude to generate a short written digest of what you've been doing, plus a set of topic tags. It also supports free-form follow-up questions against that history ("what did I look into on Tuesday?") and can export the whole session — summary, stats, and Q&A transcript — as a formatted PDF. It was originally shaped around SIWES logbook reporting, but works as a general browsing-activity summarizer for anyone who wants a quick recap of their week online.

Features

  • Selectable time range: 24 hours, 3 days, 7 days, or 30 days
  • AI-generated summary of browsing activity, written by Claude from processed history data
  • Auto-extracted topic tags for the period
  • At-a-glance stats: total visits, unique sites, top domain
  • Follow-up Q&A against the day-by-day history for that session
  • One-click PDF export with charts, a stats summary, and the full Q&A transcript

How It Works

On Analyse, the extension calls chrome.history.search() for up to 2,000 entries within the selected window, then reduces that down to the top 30 most-visited domains (by count) and the 40 most recent page titles. That reduced list — domains and titles only, no full links — is sent to Claude (claude-sonnet-4-6) with a prompt asking for a short professional summary plus 4–6 topic tags, formatted as structured output the extension can parse reliably.

The Q&A box works differently and more thoroughly: it sends the full day-grouped history — including complete URLs and titles — for that period, so Claude can answer specific questions that a domain-only summary couldn't (e.g. "which article did I read about X?"). This is a deliberate tradeoff for that one feature, not an oversight — more detail in, more useful answers out — but it does mean Q&A is more data-hungry than the main summary. See Privacy & Data Handling below.

PDF export runs entirely client-side using jsPDF, with a custom markdown-to-PDF renderer built to handle the structured output Claude returns — tables, headings, bullet lists, and stat cards are drawn directly rather than dumped as plain text.

Permissions & Architecture

Manifest V3, minimal permission surface

The extension requests exactly two permissions — history and storage — plus a single host permission scoped to https://api.anthropic.com/. No tabs, no activeTab, no broad host access, no cookie access. It can read your browsing history and make one outbound network call, and nothing else.

There's no backend server. The extension calls the Anthropic API directly from the browser using a user-supplied API key, which is stored in chrome.storage.local — device-local, not synced, not sent anywhere except as an auth header on requests to Anthropic. Going server-direct like this removes a middle party that could see your data or key, but it also means the key lives in browser storage rather than a managed server-side vault — a reasonable tradeoff for a personal-use tool, less so if this were ever shipped to other users at scale.

Privacy & Data Handling

Most of what the extension does works from domain names and page titles, not full links: the main Analyse summary only ever sends a domain-and-title list to Claude, never a raw URL. The Q&A feature is the exception — to answer a specific question about your browsing, it sends full URLs and titles for that day, since a domain list alone isn't enough detail to answer "what was that article again?" So the accurate statement is: your raw browsing data stays on your device unless a feature specifically needs more detail to do its job — the Analyse summary never needs it and never gets it; the Q&A feature does, by design. No analytics, no third-party tracking, no data collection beyond what's sent to Claude to generate a response.

Limitations & Next Steps

The clearest next step is closing the gap between the two features: the Q&A path could be redesigned to work from a redacted or domain-level view of history by default, falling back to full detail only when a question genuinely requires it, rather than sending the full day's URLs on every query. Beyond that: a lightweight backend so the API key isn't held in browser storage at all, a way to exclude specific sites or domains from analysis entirely, and handling for very high-volume history (the current 2,000-item cap on chrome.history.search() means a genuinely heavy browsing day could get truncated before it reaches the summarizer).
